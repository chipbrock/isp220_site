# Lesson 3: What Can We Know, and How?

> Science is a thing. Different from other things. How?

> This is a text about physics. If you look at other textbooks on physics they always start with an obligatory discussion on the hows and whys of science. Everyone has his or her soapbox and well, this is mine!

> Of course, my main QS&BB mission is to help you to learn some modern physics. But I'd also like for you to appreciate how science really functions in our time and how it's evolved from it's beginnings during the lifetimes of Copernicus and Newton, the timeframe affectionately looked back upon as that of the "Scientific Revolution."
> Revolution is an overused word, but here it works. Following Isaac Newton's remarkable achievements, new facts were learned, but also society realigned itself with the universe. Before Newton our ancestors believed in magic and superstition but after him, 18th century society expected that everything could be understood through reason and the Age of Enlightenment was the outcome. A direct line from humbug to knowledge, through physics!<br><br> 
Today we are surrounded by public issues that are either directly science itself, indirectly rely on science for answers, or are blamed on science as a cause. Yet as I watch the debates around me, I'm convinced that the TV news version of science is not the science that I know. Some of this is a misunderstanding about what science does and can do, but some is just confusion about words that have everyday meanings and precisely scientific uses. I want to point out how physics evolved from our current perspective and I'll do that by contrasting the complicated way that everyday words and concepts do much different jobs when they're working for science. 

I’ve been a scientist for more than four decades and so if asked, you would think that sure, I can tell you what “science” is, right? Well, it’s…subtle. There are all of these concepts that sound scientific and often use everyday words that muddy the waters, so to speak. In this section, I’ll attack this “what is science” question by picking on some of the more complicated ideas. Let me start with some bumper-sticker statements that I believe all of my colleagues would agree to. Or not. We argue a lot.

1. There are no Scientific Laws. 
2. Facts change and there is no scientific Truth.
3. It is not possible to test a theory. We test models.
4. There is a reality “out there” and our understanding of it continuously improves.
5. Science is what scientists do.
6. Scientific knowledge is a unique kind of knowledge.

Squirming yet?
